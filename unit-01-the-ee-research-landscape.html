Scope of this unit

What counts as research in electrical engineering; research versus design project versus product; the two-phase model of this course; problem formulation from topic to answerable research question; the IEEE publication ecosystem and how a paper actually reaches IEEE Xplore; the IEEE Code of Ethics and research integrity.

Worked Example UnitThis unit is written out in full as the pattern for Units 2–12. Every other unit page follows the same section order and the same section IDs.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, the student should be able to:

  1. Distinguish a research study from a design project and from a product, and state which of the three a given undertaking is.
  2. Convert a broad topic into a research question that is answerable with the evidence a team can actually produce in fifteen weeks.
  3. Explain the two-phase model of this course and describe how the Phase 1 manuscript justifies the Phase 2 prototype.
  4. Describe how a paper reaches IEEE Xplore, and name the gates it passes through on the way.
  5. Apply the IEEE Code of Ethics to authorship, data reporting, and the disclosed use of generative AI.
  6. Read an IEEE paper structurally — identify its claim, its evidence, and its gap — using the paper-anatomy worksheet.

Lecture Discussion

1.1 What counts as research in electrical engineering

A great deal of excellent engineering is not research. Wiring a building to code, repairing a switchgear, or assembling a well-documented kit are all valuable acts of engineering, and none of them produce new knowledge. Research is distinguished by one thing: at the end of it, something is known that was not known before, and the knowing is defensible — someone else, following the same method, would arrive at the same place.

In electrical engineering that new knowledge usually takes one of four shapes. It may be a measurement nobody had made, such as the true harmonic profile of a particular class of load under Philippine grid conditions. It may be a method that performs better than the one before it, judged on a stated metric. It may be an artifact that demonstrates a capability at a scale or in a context where it had not been demonstrated. Or it may be a synthesis — a review or scientometric study that maps what a field has and has not established, which is exactly what Phase 1 of this course produces.

The One-Sentence TestIf you cannot complete the sentence “Before this work, nobody had shown that ______”, you do not yet have a research project. You may have a very good design project, and that is a different thing.

1.2 Research, design project, and product — three different animals

This course requires the first and the second together. Phase 2 produces a working artifact — but the artifact only earns its marks when it has been measured against something trustworthy and reported honestly. A prototype that switches on and blinks is a design outcome. A prototype whose readings have been placed alongside a calibrated reference across thirty paired measurements, with the disagreement quantified, is research.

1.3 The two-phase model of this course

Fifteen weeks is not much time, and the usual failure mode of a research course is a team that spends ten weeks reading and five weeks panicking. The two-phase structure prevents that by making the reading itself produce a deliverable.

  • Phase 1 (Weeks 1–8) — ideate, write and defend the research project proposal, then convert the proposal's literature foundation into a review or scientometric paper and submit it to an IEEE venue indexed in IEEE Xplore. The literature review stops being homework and becomes a publication.
  • Phase 2 (Weeks 9–15) — take the gap that the Phase 1 paper quantified, turn it into requirements, build the prototype, validate it against a reference, and write the full IEEE article defended in Week 15.

The connection between the two is not decorative. The Phase 1 paper's closing gap statement becomes the Phase 2 article's opening paragraph, and its comparison tables become the related-work section. Teams that write a vague Phase 1 paper discover in Week 9 that they have nothing to build toward.

1.4 From topic to research question

A topic is a subject area. A research question is something a specific piece of work can answer. The distance between them is where most projects are won or lost.

StageExampleWhat is still wrong
Topic“Smart energy monitoring.”Not a question. No scope, no claim, no way to fail.
Narrowed topic“Low-cost energy monitoring for small commercial buildings.”Better bounded, still not answerable.
Weak question“Can we build an energy monitor using an ESP32?”The answer is known to be yes. Nothing is learned.
Workable question“How closely does a clamp-CT energy logger built from general-purpose components agree with a revenue-grade reference meter across the 0–100 A range under distorted current waveforms?”Answerable, falsifiable, and it tells you exactly what to measure.

Notice what the workable question does. It names the artifact, the comparison, the range, and the condition. It can come out badly — the agreement might be poor — and a poor result reported honestly is still a contribution, because the next team will know.

Objectives Follow the QuestionOnce the question is right, the objectives write themselves, and each one carries a number: “achieve ≤ 5% MAPE against the reference across 0–100 A” is an objective. “Develop a smart energy monitoring system” is a wish.

1.5 The IEEE publication ecosystem and how papers reach IEEE Xplore

IEEE Xplore is a digital library, not a venue. Nothing is submitted “to Xplore.” A paper arrives there because it was accepted at an IEEE-sponsored conference or by an IEEE journal that publishes into it. Choosing the venue is therefore the first practical decision, and it governs the page limit, the template, and the deadline that the rest of the semester bends around.

  1. Select a venue whose scope genuinely matches the work, and confirm that its proceedings are published in IEEE Xplore. Read the call for papers, not the conference's marketing page.
  2. Download the author kit — the official Word or LaTeX template, the page limit, and the formatting rules. Write inside the template from the first draft; retrofitting a paper into it later wastes a week.
  3. Submit and be peer reviewed. Reviewers assess novelty, method, evidence, and clarity. Reviews may take longer than this entire semester, which is why the course grades submission readiness rather than acceptance.
  4. Clear the similarity check at or below the institutional threshold, with no single source over the allowed share.
  5. Pass IEEE PDF eXpress — PDF 1.4 or later, all fonts embedded or subset, no password or security settings, and no bookmarks, links, crop marks, or timestamps.
  6. Sign the copyright form and present the paper. Most IEEE conferences require an author to actually present; a no-show paper is commonly withdrawn from the proceedings and never reaches Xplore.
Predatory VenuesIf a “conference” promises acceptance within days, lists an implausibly broad scope, cannot show a past proceedings record in Xplore, or solicits by mass email, it is not a venue — it is an invoice. Every target venue in this course is approved by the Course Professor before submission.

1.6 Ethics, integrity, and the disclosed use of AI

The IEEE Code of Ethics commits engineers, among other things, to hold paramount the safety and welfare of the public, to be honest and realistic in stating claims based on available data, to reject bribery, to credit properly the contributions of others, and to treat all persons fairly. Three of those bear directly on this course.

  • Honest claims from available data. The single most serious offence in this course is a fabricated or beautified measurement. The entire value of Phase 2 rests on the claim that the reported numbers came from the reported instrument on the reported day.
  • Proper credit. Authorship belongs to those who contributed intellectually and can defend the work. Authorship is agreed in writing at Week 5 and reconfirmed at Week 14. Generative AI systems are never authors.
  • Safety of the public. A device intended for a clinic, a barangay, or a plant floor carries obligations that a bench demonstration does not. Safety review is part of the design, not an afterthought.

Generative AI may be used in this course for language editing, code scaffolding, and literature triage. Every such use is disclosed in the manuscript's AI-use statement. AI-generated text presented as the team's own analysis, and AI-generated citations that do not exist, are treated as academic dishonesty — and fabricated references are trivially detected, because the reviewer will try to read them.

Worked Example — Taking One Topic All the Way Down

A team arrives with the topic “IoT for agriculture.” Watch what happens as each layer is stripped away.

MoveResult
Observe, do not assumeThe team visits a rice-drying yard in Rizal. Drying is judged by hand and by feel. Over-dried grain cracks and loses milling yield; under-dried grain spoils. The operator owns no moisture meter — the nearest one is at the co-operative, forty minutes away.
Name the real painNot “no internet.” The pain is no timely measurement at the point of decision.
POV statement“A small-yard drying operator needs a way to know grain moisture at the moment of turning, because the decision is made hourly and by feel, but existing meters are centralized, costly, and too slow to consult.”
How Might WeHow might we put a trustworthy moisture reading in the operator's hand within seconds, without training and without connectivity?
Research question“How closely does a handheld capacitive grain-moisture probe built from general-purpose components agree with the oven-drying reference method for palay across the 12–22% moisture range, and how repeatable is it across operators?”
Phase 1 paperA scientometric study of low-cost grain-moisture sensing: which sensing modalities dominate the literature, how their reported accuracies cluster, which crops and which regions are covered, and — the payload — which combination of modality, crop, and validation method has not been reported.
Phase 2 prototypeThe handheld probe, calibrated against oven-drying, validated with at least thirty paired measurements spanning the range, reported with MAE, RMSE, Bland–Altman limits of agreement, and inter-operator repeatability.
The claim at defence“Before this work, nobody had reported the agreement of a capacitive handheld probe against the oven-drying reference for Philippine palay across the operating range, with inter-operator repeatability. We report it, and here is where it holds and where it does not.”
What ChangedNothing about the technology got more sophisticated between the first row and the last. What changed is that there is now a claim that can be wrong, a reference that can settle it, and a number that will decide.

Studio Activity — Anatomy of a Paper

Teams of three to four are formed and registered in this session. Each team then dissects one real IEEE paper.

  1. Search IEEE Xplore for a conference or journal paper published in the last three years in an area the team finds interesting. It must report a built artifact with measured results.
  2. Read it once straight through without notes. Then read it a second time filling in the worksheet below.
  3. Complete the paper-anatomy worksheet: the venue and year; the one-sentence claim; the evidence offered for that claim; the reference or baseline used; the stated limitations; and the gap the authors say remains.
  4. Find the sentence in the introduction where the authors state their gap, and the sentence in the conclusion where they state what remains. Copy both out verbatim.
  5. Present in three minutes: “This paper claims ____, proves it by ____, and admits it cannot ____.”
Why This ExerciseReading structurally rather than linearly is the single most transferable skill in this unit. By Week 6 each team will screen well over a hundred papers, and there is no time to read any of them twice.

Self-Check Quiz

Five short items for the student's own use. Not graded.

  1. In one sentence, what makes a piece of engineering work research rather than a design project?
  2. A team proposes: “Can we build a solar-powered water level sensor using LoRa?” What is wrong with this as a research question, and how would you repair it?
  3. A paper is “submitted to IEEE Xplore.” Correct the statement.
  4. Name three gates a conference paper must pass between acceptance and appearing in IEEE Xplore.
  5. A teammate suggests removing three outlying measurements because “they make the graph look bad.” Which specific commitment in the IEEE Code of Ethics does this violate, and what is the correct action?
Answer key
  1. It produces new, defensible knowledge — a claim supported by evidence that someone else could reproduce — rather than merely a working artifact.
  2. The answer is already known to be yes, so nothing is learned and the work cannot fail informatively. Repair it by adding a comparison, a range, and a condition: “How closely does a LoRa-linked ultrasonic level sensor agree with a calibrated pressure transducer across a 0–3 m range during rainfall?”
  3. Papers are not submitted to Xplore. They are submitted to an IEEE conference or journal; if accepted and processed, the publisher deposits them into IEEE Xplore.
  4. Any three of: peer review and acceptance; similarity check within threshold; formatting to the official IEEE template within the page limit; IEEE PDF eXpress validation; signed copyright form; presentation at the conference.
  5. It violates the commitment to be honest and realistic in stating claims based on available data. Outliers are investigated and reported with the reason for any exclusion stated in the methodology — never removed silently for appearance.

HOTS Test (20 items)

Higher-order thinking items — analysis, evaluation, and creation rather than recall. These may be drawn upon for quizzes and for the Preliminary Examination.

  1. A team replicates a published circuit exactly and confirms it works. Which is the strongest justification for calling this research?
    1. The circuit was difficult to build.
    2. The replication was performed under conditions the original paper did not test, and the result is compared against it.
    3. The team wrote it up in IEEE format.
    4. No one at the University had built it before.
  2. Which objective is best written for a validation study?
    1. To develop an accurate and reliable measuring device.
    2. To achieve a mean absolute percentage error of ≤ 5% against a calibrated reference over 0–200 A.
    3. To help the community through innovative technology.
    4. To design, implement, and evaluate an IoT-based system.
  3. A proposal's gap statement reads: “Few studies have explored this area.” The strongest objection is that it —
    1. is grammatically weak.
    2. makes an unquantified claim that a reproducible search could refute.
    3. should appear in the conclusion instead.
    4. needs more citations attached.
  4. Phase 1 exists chiefly to —
    1. give students practice at writing.
    2. fill the weeks before hardware arrives.
    3. produce the quantified evidence that justifies what Phase 2 builds.
    4. satisfy the requirement for two publications.
  5. Which finding, honestly reported, still constitutes a contribution?
    1. The prototype agreed with the reference only below 40% of the range, and the cause was identified.
    2. The prototype worked perfectly, but no reference was available.
    3. The prototype was not finished, but the design is sound.
    4. The prototype matched a simulation exactly.
  6. A conference guarantees acceptance in 72 hours and lists scope covering all of engineering, medicine, and management. The correct inference is that it —
    1. is efficient and worth targeting for a fast turnaround.
    2. shows signs of a predatory venue and must not be used.
    3. is acceptable if it charges no fee.
    4. is acceptable if a past paper appears anywhere online.
  7. Two teams measure the same quantity with the same device and get different results. The most useful next step is to —
    1. average the two results.
    2. report the one closer to the reference.
    3. quantify repeatability and reproducibility, and report both.
    4. repeat until the results agree.
  8. A student drafts a section with an AI assistant, verifies every claim, and rewrites it in their own words. Under this course's policy they must —
    1. do nothing further; the text is now theirs.
    2. disclose the use in the manuscript's AI-use statement.
    3. list the AI system as a contributing author.
    4. remove the section entirely.
  9. Which is the clearest sign that a “research question” is actually a design task in disguise?
    1. It names a specific microcontroller.
    2. Its answer is already known to be yes before any work is done.
    3. It mentions cost.
    4. It is more than one sentence long.
  10. The best reason to fix the target venue in Week 5 rather than Week 8 is that the venue determines —
    1. the marking scheme for the course.
    2. the page limit, template, and deadline the rest of the work must fit.
    3. which team members are listed as authors.
    4. whether ethics clearance is required.
  11. An abstract claims “high accuracy” without a number. The most substantive criticism is that —
    1. abstracts should not contain numbers.
    2. the claim is unfalsifiable as written and cannot be compared to prior work.
    3. it exceeds the word limit.
    4. accuracy belongs only in the results section.
  12. A team's device disagrees with the reference by a fixed offset across the whole range. This most likely indicates —
    1. a random error requiring more samples.
    2. a systematic error correctable by calibration.
    3. a faulty reference instrument.
    4. an unsuitable sensing modality.
  13. Which pairing correctly matches contribution type to output?
    1. Synthesis → a new circuit topology.
    2. Measurement → a scientometric map of a field.
    3. Synthesis → a review paper establishing what a field has and has not shown.
    4. Method → a photograph of a working prototype.
  14. The strongest argument for grading Phase 1 on submission rather than acceptance is that —
    1. acceptance rates are unfair.
    2. peer review runs on a timetable far longer than one semester.
    3. reviewers are inconsistent.
    4. students should not be discouraged.
  15. A proposal states its significance as “This study will benefit students, teachers, and future researchers.” The defect is that it —
    1. is too short.
    2. names beneficiaries without a mechanism of benefit.
    3. omits the funding source.
    4. should be in the introduction.
  16. Which comparison best establishes that a method is better?
    1. It succeeded in every trial the team ran.
    2. It outperformed a clearly stated baseline on a pre-declared metric under the same conditions.
    3. It was faster to build than the alternative.
    4. It used more recent components.
  17. A team wants to skip the reference instrument because none is available on campus. The best remaining option is to —
    1. report the device's own readings as ground truth.
    2. use an accepted reference method, standard, or ground-truth dataset, and state the substitution and its limits.
    3. omit validation and expand the design section.
    4. compare against a second unit of the same design.
  18. Authorship on the Phase 2 article is best decided —
    1. by seniority within the team.
    2. in writing at Week 5 and reconfirmed at Week 14, on the basis of intellectual contribution.
    3. after the article is accepted.
    4. equally among all enrolled students.
  19. The Phase 1 paper's most important structural obligation is to —
    1. cite as many sources as possible.
    2. include at least six figures.
    3. close with a research-gap statement that names the Phase 2 work as the next step.
    4. use the same template as the Phase 2 article.
  20. Design the strongest one-sentence contribution claim you could make for a project measuring transformer loading in a distribution feeder, given that a calibrated reference clamp meter is available and prior work covers only laboratory conditions. Write the sentence, then name the one measurement that would refute it.
Answer key

1 B · 2 B · 3 B · 4 C · 5 A · 6 B · 7 C · 8 B · 9 B · 10 B · 11 B · 12 B · 13 C · 14 B · 15 B · 16 B · 17 B · 18 B · 19 C

20 — Open response. A strong answer names the artifact, the field condition, the reference, and the range, for example: “A clamp-CT loading monitor agrees with a calibrated reference clamp meter to within X% across the feeder's normal loading range under field conditions, where prior work reports agreement only under laboratory conditions.” The refuting measurement is a paired field comparison across the range showing disagreement beyond X% — the answer must name a measurement that could actually come out the other way.

HOTS Reinforcement Assignment

Submitted individually before the Week 2 session.

  1. Three topics, three questions (9 items). Choose three areas of electrical engineering that interest you. For each, write the topic, a weak question, and a workable research question, then state in one line what would have to be measured to answer it.
  2. Classification drill (5 items). For five undertakings supplied in class, classify each as research, design project, or product, and justify the classification in one sentence.
  3. Venue reconnaissance (3 items). Find three IEEE conferences or journals in your area of interest whose proceedings appear in IEEE Xplore. For each, record the scope, the page limit, the template, and the submission deadline.
  4. Ethics case (3 items). Three short scenarios are supplied in class. For each, identify the ethical commitment at stake, the correct action, and the consequence of the wrong one.
AssessmentScored on the sharpness of the research questions and the accuracy of the venue records, not on length. One page is enough if it is the right page.

Futures Essay (3 items)

Approximately 300 words each. Argue a position and defend it with evidence rather than assertion.

  1. If generative AI can draft a competent literature review in minutes, what is left that makes a human-authored review paper worth publishing? Argue for the specific contribution that survives.
  2. Peer review is slow, unpaid, and inconsistent, yet it remains the gate to IEEE Xplore. Propose one concrete change to how engineering research is validated, and state honestly what your change would cost the field.
  3. Much electrical engineering research is produced in well-resourced laboratories and then applied in places nothing like them. What would it take for research produced in a Philippine university to set the terms of a field rather than follow them?
