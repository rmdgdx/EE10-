Course Description

This course carries an electrical engineering research project from the first spark of an idea to a defended, publication-ready body of work within a single 15-week semester. It is organized as two linked studies executed back to back. In Phase 1 (Weeks 1–8), teams run a structured design-thinking and ideation sprint, write and defend a research project proposal, and then convert the proposal's literature foundation into a rigorous review or scientometric (bibliometric) paper written in IEEE double-column format and submitted to an IEEE conference or journal indexed in IEEE Xplore. In Phase 2 (Weeks 9–15), the research gap identified and quantified in Phase 1 is answered by building, calibrating, validating, and demonstrating a working prototype — an instrument, a handheld device, an embedded or edge-AI system, or a companion application — which is then written up as a full IEEE article and defended before a panel.

The course is deliberately technological but restrained in scale. Teams are held to a simple architecture and a single well-defended claim, which forces the innovation into sensing strategy, signal processing, embedded intelligence, and validation rigor rather than into elaborate hardware. The guiding standard is simple to build, hard to argue with, and demonstrably useful: a device or system that a barangay, a clinic, a farm cooperative, a small plant, or a distribution utility could actually deploy, benchmarked against a calibrated reference and reported with honest uncertainty.

Five Course Thrusts(i) Design thinking and structured ideation for high-impact engineering innovation · (ii) The research project proposal and its defense · (iii) Phase 1 — scientometric/review paper submitted to IEEE Xplore · (iv) Phase 2 — prototype execution, calibration, and validation · (v) The full IEEE article and the final panel defense.
Two Papers, One SemesterPhase 1 output: a 6–8 page IEEE-format review or scientometric paper mapping the field, quantifying the research front, and stating the gap — submitted by Week 8. Phase 2 output: a full IEEE article reporting the prototype's design, validation against a reference standard, and impact — camera-ready by Week 14 and defended in Week 15. Phase 1 is the argument that the problem matters; Phase 2 is the evidence that the team solved a piece of it.
Note on IndexingIEEE Xplore is a digital library, not a venue. Papers reach it by being accepted at an IEEE-sponsored conference or an IEEE journal that publishes there. Each team therefore selects a specific target venue with the Course Professor's approval, follows that venue's author kit and page limit, and clears IEEE PDF eXpress before submission. Grading is based on submission readiness and proof of submission, never on acceptance, which falls outside the semester.

Learning Outcomes

Upon completion of this course, the students should be able to:

Knowledge

  1. (CO1) Explain the engineering research process — problem formulation, review of related literature, research design, ethical clearance, and scholarly dissemination — and apply IEEE authorship, citation, and publication-ethics standards.
  2. (CO2) Apply scientometric and bibliometric methods — reproducible database queries, PRISMA-guided screening, performance analysis, and science mapping — to characterize a research field and defend a quantified research gap.
  3. (CO3) Apply design thinking and the engineering design process to generate, screen, and select an innovative yet buildable electrical engineering solution under realistic safety, standards, and availability constraints.

Skills

  1. (CO4) Design, fabricate, program, and integrate a functional prototype — instrument, handheld device, embedded/edge-AI system, or companion application — using modern engineering tools for schematic capture, PCB layout, firmware, simulation, and data analysis.
  2. (CO5) Calibrate and validate the prototype against a calibrated reference instrument or accepted standard, and report accuracy, precision, repeatability, agreement, and measurement uncertainty honestly.
  3. (CO6) Produce two IEEE-formatted manuscripts — a Phase 1 review/scientometric paper and a Phase 2 full article — and defend the work orally before a technical panel with a clear, evidence-led presentation and a live prototype demonstration.

Values and Research

  1. (CO7) Work productively as a member and leader of a project team: plan, procure, document, and deliver against firm milestones with a complete engineering logbook and traceable version control.
  2. Uphold research integrity — honest reporting of results, no fabricated or beautified data, proper attribution, disclosed use of generative AI tools, and similarity-checked writing. Ethics
  3. Practice electrical safety and regulatory compliance under the Philippine Electrical Code and the IEEE Code of Ethics, and evaluate each design for its societal, economic, environmental, and sustainability impact on Philippine communities.
  4. Embody the RTU core values — Integrity, Innovativeness, Excellence, Nationalism, and Inclusivity — by directing engineering innovation toward problems that matter locally.

D. Novelty check protocol (mandatory before the proposal defense)

  1. Write the exact Boolean query used, with database, date of search, and field tags. Run it in IEEE Xplore, Scopus, and Google Scholar; record hit counts.
  2. Read the ten most-cited and the ten most-recent results. Tabulate what each one measured, built, or achieved, and under what conditions.
  3. State the gap in one sentence of the form: “Existing work does X but has not yet done Y under condition Z.”
  4. Write the novelty statement as exactly three claims — what is new in the approach, what is new in the implementation, and what is new in the evidence the team will produce.
  5. If more than roughly thirty near-identical works already exist, the concept is not dead — but the team must differentiate along a named innovation vector and say so explicitly in the proposal.

E. Anti-patterns — concepts that will not be approved

Automatic Rejection
  • “IoT-based [anything] monitoring system” with a dashboard but no measurement validation against a reference and no research question.
  • Line-following robots, obstacle-avoiding cars, RFID attendance systems, and other exercises with no novelty vector and no measurable claim.
  • Straight re-implementations of a published project with no comparative baseline, no improvement claim, and no new evidence.
  • Concepts depending on parts with lead times beyond two weeks, or on a single unobtainable component with no second source.
  • Work at voltages, currents, RF power, laser classes, or chemical hazards beyond the laboratory's safety envelope and the team's certification.
  • Human-subject data collection without ethics clearance, or any handling of personal data outside the Data Privacy Act of 2012.
  • Simulation-only submissions where the proposal promised hardware. The prototype must physically exist and run at the Week 15 defense.

Scoping RuleOne measurand, one claim, one validation. A device that measures four things badly loses to a device that measures one thing well and proves it against a calibrated reference. Breadth is not a substitute for evidence, and panels reward the paper that can be defended.

Phase 1 — The Proposal and the IEEE Xplore Review / Scientometric Paper

Phase 1 runs from Week 1 to Week 8 and produces two documents: the research project proposal, defended in Week 5, and the Phase 1 manuscript — a review or scientometric paper — submitted in Week 8 to an IEEE conference or journal whose proceedings are published in IEEE Xplore. The manuscript is not a side quest. It is the formal argument that the Phase 2 prototype is needed, and its concluding gap statement becomes the introduction of the Phase 2 article.

Non-negotiable methodological requirements

  • Reproducibility. The exact query string, database, date of search, filters, and hit counts are reported verbatim in the methodology so any reader can regenerate the corpus.
  • Corpus size. Minimum 150 records after screening for a scientometric paper; minimum 40 fully-read studies for a systematic review. Smaller corpora require written approval and a narrower, justified scope.
  • PRISMA 2020. The identification–screening–eligibility–inclusion flow diagram is mandatory in both routes, with numbers at every stage and reasons for exclusion.
  • Data hygiene. Deduplication across databases, author-name and affiliation disambiguation, and keyword harmonization are documented in a cleaning log.
  • Figures earn their space. At least six publication-grade figures, each interpreted in the text. A network diagram with no interpretation is decoration, not evidence.
  • The gap is the payload. The paper must close with a research-gap statement and a technology roadmap that names the Phase 2 prototype as the logical next step.

C. Submission pipeline (Week 8)

  1. Select the approved target venue and download its author kit; confirm the page limit, template version, and whether the venue publishes to IEEE Xplore.
  2. Format the manuscript in the official IEEE conference or journal template — double column, correct margins, embedded fonts, IEEE reference style.
  3. Complete internal peer review: each team receives two mock referee reports from other teams and responds in writing.
  4. Run the similarity check; the manuscript must be at or below the institutional threshold, with no single source exceeding the allowed share.
  5. Generate a compliant PDF and clear it through IEEE PDF eXpress: PDF 1.4 or later, all fonts embedded or subset, no password or security settings, no bookmarks, links, crop marks, or timestamps.
  6. Submit through the venue's system and archive the submission confirmation, paper ID, and a screenshot as proof of submission.
Graded on Submission, Not AcceptancePeer review runs on its own timetable, well past Week 15. Phase 1 is scored on methodological rigor, manuscript quality, template compliance, and documented proof of submission. Acceptance, when it comes, is celebrated — not graded.

Phase 2 — Prototype Execution and the Full IEEE Article

Phase 2 runs from Week 9 to Week 15. The gap quantified in Phase 1 becomes a requirements table; the requirements table becomes an architecture; the architecture becomes hardware; the hardware is calibrated and validated against a reference; and the whole chain is reported as a full IEEE article. Nothing in this phase is accepted on the strength of a claim alone — every specification must be traceable to a measurement.

C. Calibration and validation (Week 13)

This is the week that separates a project from research. The prototype's claims are tested against something the panel can trust.

  • Reference and traceability. Name the reference instrument, its make and model, its accuracy class, and its calibration status. Where no instrument exists, name the accepted standard, reference method, or ground-truth dataset used instead.
  • Calibration. Build the calibration curve across the operating range, fit and report the correction model, and state the residuals. Report before-and-after performance.
  • Paired measurement design. Minimum 30 paired observations per measurand, spanning the full range rather than clustering at one convenient point, collected under stated environmental conditions.
  • Accuracy and error. Report MAE, RMSE, MAPE, and R² with a scatter plot against the reference and a residual plot. Do not report a single average error and stop.
  • Agreement. Produce a Bland–Altman plot with bias and 95% limits of agreement — correlation alone is not agreement, and panels will ask.
  • Precision and stability. Report repeatability (%CV) from replicate runs, reproducibility across units where more than one was built, and warm-up and long-run drift.
  • Uncertainty budget. Tabulate the dominant uncertainty contributors — reference, sensor, quantization, temperature, installation — and combine them into a stated expanded uncertainty.
  • Robustness and safety. Report behavior at temperature and humidity extremes, under supply disturbance, and at input limits; document protection, fusing, and isolation.
  • Honesty clause. Failed tests, out-of-spec ranges, and unresolved anomalies are reported, not hidden. A limitation stated plainly costs almost nothing at defense; a limitation discovered by the panel costs a great deal.

  • Week 6Phase 1 corpus — registered query strings with hit counts, PRISMA 2020 flow diagram, cleaned dataset with codebook.
  • Week 7Phase 1 analysis — reproducible notebook, complete figure set (≥ 6 figures), interpretation memo.
  • Week 8Phase 1 paper submitted — IEEE-format manuscript, mock referee responses, similarity report, PDF eXpress-approved PDF, proof of submission. Phase 1
  • Week 9Phase 2 design colloquium — requirements and specifications table, system architecture, V&V plan, safety and standards review.
  • Week 10Approved Phase 2 design document and component procurement plan, alongside the Midterm Examination.
  • Week 11Bring-up demonstration — every subsystem working in isolation; logbook and repository in order.
  • Week 12Critical design review — integrated prototype running unattended for one hour; action-item log; as-built component list reconciled against the approved design.
  • Week 13Validation package — calibration report, ≥ 30 paired measurements per measurand, error and agreement analysis, uncertainty budget.
  • Week 14Camera-ready full article — similarity report, PDF eXpress-approved PDF, benchmark and performance tables. Phase 2
  • Week 15Final defense alongside the Final Examination — live prototype demonstration, panel questioning, article submission, and full project turnover.

Final defense format (Week 15)

  • Presentation — 20 minutes. Problem and evidence of its scale; the Phase 1 gap in figures; the design and the reasoning behind it; the validation results; the impact case; limitations and future work.
  • Live demonstration — 10 minutes. The prototype is switched on in the room, takes real measurements, and shows results. A video is accepted only for hazardous, field-bound, or destructive tests, and must be declared in advance.
  • Panel questioning — 15 minutes. Every team member answers. The panel probes the measurement chain, the validity of the reference, the honesty of the error analysis, and the safety of the design.
  • Turnover. Hardware, firmware and analysis repository, raw and processed datasets, schematics and PCB files, engineering logbook, and both manuscripts are submitted as one documented package.
Panel CompositionThe defense panel comprises the Course Professor as chair and at least two faculty members or industry practitioners from the electrical, electronics, or computer engineering fields. A representative of the beneficiary community, agency, or company is invited whenever the project has an identified deployment partner.

Course Policies

Attendance, Studio Work, and Examinations

Attendance and participation follow the RTU Student Handbook. Studio sessions, build sprints, and technical reviews are conducted within class hours; teams are expected to schedule additional laboratory time for fabrication and validation. The Preliminary, Midterm, and Final Examinations follow the RTU academic calendar, and special examinations are governed by University policy. Milestone deadlines are firm. Each week's deliverable is the input to the next week's work, and the Week 8 and Week 14 submission windows are governed by external venue timelines that the course cannot move. Late milestone submissions are penalized 10% per calendar day, to a maximum of three days, after which the deliverable is scored zero.

Laboratory and Electrical Safety

No student works alone on energized equipment. Work above 50 V AC or 120 V DC requires the Course Professor's written clearance and direct supervision, with appropriate personal protective equipment, lockout–tagout discipline, and a documented risk assessment filed before the first energized test. Battery charging, lithium cell handling, soldering, 3D printing, and any use of lasers or compressed gas follow the laboratory's standard operating procedures. Every design is reviewed against the Philippine Electrical Code for creepage and clearance, conductor sizing, overcurrent protection, grounding, and enclosure integrity before field testing. Unsafe work is stopped and does not earn credit.

Academic Integrity and Responsible Use of AI Tools

All manuscripts pass through similarity checking before submission. Plagiarism, data fabrication, falsification, image manipulation, and undisclosed reuse of another team's hardware, code, or results result in a failing mark for the requirement and referral under institutional policy. Fabricated measurement data is treated as the most serious offense in this course — the entire value of Phase 2 rests on the claim that the reported numbers came from the reported instrument on the reported day. Generative AI may be used for language editing, code scaffolding, and literature triage, but every such use is disclosed in the manuscript's AI-use statement, and AI systems are never listed as authors, consistent with IEEE authorship policy. AI-generated text presented as the team's own analysis, and AI-generated citations that do not exist, are treated as academic dishonesty.

Research Ethics and Data Privacy

Studies involving human participants — including usability testing, physiological measurement, interviews, and survey data — require clearance from the institutional research ethics committee before data collection begins, with informed consent documented. Personal data is collected, stored, and processed in accordance with the Data Privacy Act of 2012, with anonymization, minimization, and secure storage as defaults. Field work in communities requires the written consent of the barangay or partner organization. Datasets published alongside either manuscript are stripped of identifiers and accompanied by a data dictionary.

Intellectual Property and Project Turnover

Intellectual property arising from the project is governed by the University's IP policy. Teams are encouraged to disclose patentable outcomes to the University's technology transfer office before public disclosure; where a disclosure is filed, the Course Professor will advise on the timing of manuscript submission. Hardware purchased with team funds remains with the team after turnover, but the prototype, source code, schematics, datasets, and documentation are deposited with the University for archival, verification, and continuation by future cohorts. Authorship on both manuscripts follows IEEE criteria and is agreed in writing at Week 5, then reconfirmed at Week 14.

Consultation and Mentoring

Each team is entitled to a weekly consultation slot with the Course Professor, Dr. Ryan Manuel D. Guido, throughout the semester, with additional consultation windows during the Week 4–5 proposal period, the Week 7–8 submission push, and the Week 13–14 validation and writing period. Teams bring their engineering logbook and a written agenda to every consultation. Between consultations, teams are expected to resolve routine technical issues through datasheets, application notes, and their own experiments — the logbook of what was tried and what failed is itself a graded artifact.
